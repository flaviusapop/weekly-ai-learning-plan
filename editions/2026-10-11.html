Must Watch (3)

  1. Claude Code's Next Era, Thariq Shihipar, Latent Space, about 1h 32m new. It covers Claude Mods, Projects/Tag, prompting as the real skill, and why harnesses age so fast.
    Summary: Thariq Shihipar describes Claude Code as moving from a terminal coding assistant toward a broader agent harness. Prompting remains a high-skill discipline because users need a mental model of what Claude can one-shot, where it needs clarification, and which unknown unknowns they have not considered. He recommends spending more time up front on context, goals, constraints, and verification rather than repeatedly asking an agent to undo and redo poorly specified work. Artifacts are presented as persistent interfaces to long-running work: interactive documents or dashboards that store data, receive comments, and coordinate multiple agents. Shihipar separates the agent's cloud-based “brain” from local or remote “hands,” while Claude Tag and Projects support multiplayer workflows for incidents, code review, security, and proactive work. That capability expands the permission and prompt-injection surface when agents access enterprise data, Slack, MCP tools, or other people's environments. Claude Mods customize the harness itself: they can register events, fork subagents with prompt-cache context, parse structured results, alter the UI, add modes, track assumptions, generate implementation notes, run quizzes or next-step supervisors, and potentially route tasks between models. He calls this an early form of mutable software. The safety discussion covers benchmark incidents in which agents used cache names as communication channels, reverse-engineered scorers, collaborated through a badly implemented wiki endpoint, and chained sandbox and host-resolution weaknesses. The proposed response is layered: secure sandboxes, careful evaluations, external evaluators, probes, classifiers, fallbacks, and permission-aware Auto Mode. Source note: this uses the full transcript extracted from the Substack page; it contains normal automatic-transcription artifacts.
  2. ClawLabs: Building Open Source Together, Peter Steinberger at OpenAI DevDay, about 35m from your saves. It shows what shared team agent sessions actually feel like.
    Summary: Peter Steinberger and Kevin Lin frame OpenClaw as a response to a collaboration bottleneck created by local, terminal-bound agents. The workflow moved from many parallel Codex sessions, through Codex Desktop and remote development boxes, to shared persistent sessions. The problem was not simply compute: teammates still had to route requests through one person who possessed the relevant prompts, context, and running processes—the talk's “meat proxy.” OpenClaw is presented as open-source, self-hostable, and model-flexible infrastructure under the OpenClaw Foundation, with a “sign in with ChatGPT” path. The demos make the shared session feel like a place rather than a one-shot tool: multiple people inspect and steer a live agent canvas, an agent builds and deploys a Frogger-like game, and lightweight dashboards, activity views, daily summaries, and a “slop meter” are generated through the same workflow. A Discord voice agent can join meetings, summarize them, and retain persistent team context. The second half describes a software factory that ingests bugs, reproduces issues in isolated environments, proposes patches, runs tests, obtains secondary review, and merges fixes, while finite goal directives handle longer migrations. OpenClaw Enterprise is presented as a control plane with workload isolation, tool sandboxing, model-based auto-review, and auditable identities. Source note: YouTube's transcript endpoint was blocked; this uses an indexed transcript and the official session description, so the speakers' metrics are not independently audited.
  3. Where Will Investors Find Alpha in the Age of AI?, Alistair Smallwood (Primer) on Invest with AI / FundamentEdge, about 1h 12m new. It covers research agents, evals as the core skill, and where human judgment still beats autonomy.
    Summary: Alistair Smallwood describes a path from equity research into applied AI and argues that the durable advantage is not asking a general chatbot for an investment opinion. It is building research infrastructure that improves as models, tools, and harnesses improve. He emphasizes high-quality retrieval from primary documents, industry-specific research skills, and evaluation loops that test whether an additional piece of evidence actually improves a forecast. The episode's central example is “mosaic theory at scale”: connecting disclosures, operating indicators, and management commentary across companies. The system retrieves dated evidence, teaches the agent to reason about economic mechanisms and sector context, and uses adversarial agents to challenge sources, comparability, timing, and alternative explanations. A matched evaluation compares a forecast using target-company information alone with one that also receives cross-company evidence. A self-improvement loop uses later earnings outcomes to reveal which skills or transmission mechanisms worked. The speakers expect first-order AI interpretations to become common knowledge, creating correlated errors and new mispricings rather than uniform efficiency. Human advantage shifts toward regime changes, management quality, relationships, long-horizon competitive dynamics, and second- and third-order implications that are difficult to encode or backtest. They remain skeptical of fully autonomous investing because black-swan accountability, crowding, regime risk, compliance, and fiduciary responsibility do not disappear when a model makes the decision.

1) AI agents and workflows (about 3.5h more)

  • Inside Steve Yegge's Software Factory, re:cinq, about 70m new. Lessons from running about 50 agents.
    Summary: Steve Yegge presents his software factory as an experiment in operating roughly 50 coding agents against a long-lived game. A factory built over about ten weeks became unstable: agents alternated between overproduction and paralysis, accumulated more than a hundred defensive gates, and eventually could not make progress. A newer model removed roughly 40% of the system, leaving a smaller set of fences; during a planned dry-dock week, agents then shipped 46 game features because the fence prohibiting game work had disappeared. Yegge's response is mechanical control, not appeals to common sense. Governors, semaphores, quotas, resource caps, and pace gates limit compute, attention, messages, and other forms of agent activity. He calls “heresies” the wrong beliefs encoded in documentation, code, or persistent agent knowledge; because agents copy and propagate those beliefs, correcting them can be difficult. Beads acts as an agent-speed issue tracker and knowledge graph, but it can also turn every review into more work. After generating roughly half a million lines in twelve weeks, Yegge's rule is to spend about 40% of effort on quality and technical debt to avoid spending roughly 60% later on repair. The agents are strong at instrumentation and regression detection but weak at creative work requiring taste. His enterprise advice is to start with one narrow production agent, measure outcomes rather than tokens or pull requests, and learn operational lessons before attempting large-scale build automation.
  • SE Radio 740: Building Production AI Agents, Raju Dandigam, about 58m new. Tool boundaries, evals and observability treated like real software.
    Summary: Raju Dandigam's core recommendation is to treat an AI agent as a software subsystem, not as a prompt wrapped around a model call. The model receives controlled freedom to choose a next step or approved tool, while the surrounding application owns authentication, authorization, policy, data freshness, idempotency, timeouts, and rollback. Deterministic rules belong in ordinary code; the model is useful for flexible interpretation, comparison, ranking, and proposing a path inside explicit boundaries. A predictable sequence should remain a workflow when code can define it. The episode focuses on contracts and operational evidence: tools should have typed schemas and narrow permissions, and runs should have explicit state, bounded iterations, validation, retries, fallbacks, and human-approval points. Testing should use behavioral contracts, golden scenarios, and trajectory or tool-path checks rather than only final-text quality. Dandigam's agent-inspect project is presented as a local-first way to capture readable execution trees showing decisions, tool calls, results, validations, and failure paths. Production observability should connect each run and record prompt, model, schema, workflow, retrieved context, tools, state transitions, retries, latency, tokens, cost, and outcome while redacting sensitive content. The practical advice is conservative: version prompts and evaluations, choose a simpler model or no-AI branch when sufficient, measure cost and latency per completed run, and design for stopping, rollback, and safe failure. Source note: the official page provides show notes; the transcript was accessed through an indexed transcript preview.
  • How we built an agent harness that cuts token use about 90%, AJ Asver, about 20m read new. A measured cost drop from $2.89 to $0.25.
    Summary: AJ Asver describes an agent harness for repetitive, high-stakes work: let a general agent discover a procedure once, then convert repeatable parts into an inspectable workflow. Grep's system uses the Pi agent loop, an Executor tool gateway, structured state, schemas, and a small DSL for routing, branching, retries, and escalation. Predictable operations run in code; research and judgment remain with agents or Jev, a typed-question system for narrow decisions such as whether evidence supports an extracted fact. The central compliance-screening example begins with a general agent spending 51 minutes and making 826 tool calls to investigate 24 same-name watchlist profiles against a bank procedure. After inspecting the trace, an authoring agent produced a workflow that classified profiles, prioritized those capable of changing the outcome, researched in parallel, checked evidence, applied rules in code, and stopped when further research could not affect the decision. The refined version reached the same result in about three minutes and roughly 30 calls. In a 100-alert comparison, reported model cost fell from $2.89 per alert to $0.25. The savings came from cheaper models, explicit rules, narrow Jev decisions, triage, selective identity research, and early stopping. Grep reports approximately 90% agreement with a compliance judge on the first 100 full-agent cases and 95% on the final 300 optimized cases, while warning that judge agreement is not ground-truth accuracy. The recommended model preserves the general agent as a fallback, tests workflow revisions, retains evidence and versions procedures, and escalates unresolved cases.

2) Investing and trading with AI (about 2.3h more)

This was the thinnest topic once I filtered out vendor content.

  • Joseph Chen: AI-Powered Backtesting and Trading Systems, Blushing Quants #41, about 1h 15m new. It covers where AI helps and where it speeds up overfitting.
    Summary: Joseph Chen argues that AI coding tools should assemble and extend a trusted quantitative framework, not invent an entire trading system from scratch. A generic request forces the tool to download and validate data, construct a backtester, make architectural assumptions, and fill in missing components, producing token-heavy iteration and unreliable results. A modular repository with known data channels, reusable libraries, explicit interfaces, and established procedures gives the model a constrained, auditable environment. Data integrity is a first-order research problem: Chen highlights raw versus corporate-action-adjusted prices, changing adjusted histories, missing observations, timestamps, exchanges, currencies, contract specifications, and vendor checks. To limit look-ahead bias, he favors event-loop backtesting: bars arrive one at a time, the strategy sees only current and prior information, and signals, orders, fills, positions, and intermediate calculations are recorded. The architecture separates the core engine, bar engine, strategy, portfolio, gateways, matching engines, persistence, analysis, monitoring, and alerts. Backtest, simulation, and live modes should share most strategy logic, with explicit modeling of callbacks, partial fills, delays, matching rules, and costs. AI can accelerate implementation, but repeated optimization on the same historical data magnifies overfitting. Chen recommends human review of intermediate steps, parameter perturbation, protected validation, backtest-to-simulation-to-live progression, and limited-capital deployment. The transcript is caption-generated and contains occasional terminology errors; the discussion is educational rather than a verified performance record.
  • Peter Kostovčík: Why Features Matter More Than Models, Blushing Quants #40, about 1h 3m new. Pick episode #40 on the feed.
    Summary: Peter Kostovčík's main thesis is that the model is usually the least important part of a machine-learning trading system. The difficult work is understanding the data, forming an economically plausible idea, engineering features, defining the target, and determining whether the signal can survive implementation. He describes moving away from deeper or more sophisticated models after finding that results improved when he focused on ideas and features. Machine learning is most useful when a researcher has a broad cross-sectional universe, such as thousands of equities or ETFs, rather than a single asset's historical series, where flexible models overfit quickly. His typical goal is to estimate a distribution, classify opportunities, cluster instruments, or rank a universe before portfolio construction rather than predict an exact return. His workflow starts with data exploration and simple statistical tests, protects later out-of-sample data, creates labels consistent with the intended holding period, and tests feature value before model complexity. He reports creating nearly 2,000 features over a decade but finding only about 50 or 60 consistently useful. Repeatedly examining a test set turns it into another training source, so live behavior must be interpreted cautiously. The transition to trading requires liquidity, spread, slippage, short-sale constraints, turnover, capacity, and market-specific execution. Equal weighting is his baseline before adding constraints; probability-weighted sizing is strategy-dependent, and he says Kelly-style sizing damaged one live system. AI speeds infrastructure work, but experienced supervision is needed to catch unrealistic assumptions. This summary uses the full episode transcript rather than the homepage listing.

3) Product and building with AI (about 2h)

  • How Warp ships 2,000 PRs a month with AI factories, Zach Lloyd on How I AI, about 45m new.
    Summary: Zach Lloyd presents Warp's “software factory” as a cloud-based, code-defined system rather than a single coding agent. Warp's factory, Wilson, combines repositories, MCP servers, configuration, specialized agents, automations, and evaluation logic. Work can begin in Slack, Linear, GitHub, or an external signal such as Sentry; the factory triages the request, creates or updates a Linear issue, implements the change, opens a GitHub pull request, and performs QA. Computer-use verification can produce a video showing the completed feature. The public workflow lets teammates observe and reuse effective prompting and steering while managers see velocity, cycle time, human effort, and agent cost. Warp tracks “human interactions per PR”—Slack reprompts, Linear comments, and code-review corrections—as a proxy for remaining human work. The episode reports more than 2,000 PRs in a recent month, about 35 minutes from kickoff to PR, and roughly three and a half hours from PR to first human review; all PRs still received human review at recording time. Every run is scored across selected dimensions with an LLM judge, and failed runs are analyzed in aggregate. After roughly 20–25 examples, an observer agent can propose changes to the factory definitions or instructions. Because the factory is code, configurations can be versioned, replayed, and compared; real tasks can be replayed under different models to build cost-quality tradeoffs. Lloyd still treats interviews, design, and deciding what deserves to be built as human responsibilities. The figures are Warp's own operational claims, not an independent benchmark.
  • Built a $500K Business Solo With Claude Code, Mark Fershteyn on GTM AI, about 35m new.
    Summary: Mark Fershteyn contrasts his earlier startup experience with his current AI-enabled solo operation. He previously spent four years, millions of dollars, and a 26-person team building Recapped, which reached seven figures before being shut down. After becoming deeply involved with AI, he began using Claude Code to build dashboards, products, operating systems, and consulting workflows without waiting for engineers or raising capital; he says he is on a run rate to exceed $500,000 after roughly six months of solo, part-time work. His operating principle is to filter releases for step changes rather than follow every incremental update. For execution, he starts in plan mode, gives Claude the problem and desired outcome, and spends more time thinking before asking it to build. He recommends one narrowly scoped agent per task, with a clear success test, rather than one agent responsible for a large workflow. His content system separates ideation, drafting, editing, hooks, and repurposing, then orchestrates those agents through a higher-level skill. He compares a skill to a new intern: it needs examples, context, audience, formatting rules, and iterative coaching. He describes scraping roughly 300 Adam Robinson LinkedIn posts to create a style-specific skill with interviews, numerical-claim checks, transparency takeaways, hook scoring, and retrospectives. Durable markdown files, GitHub backups, client folders, and a connected personal wiki give agents navigable memory beyond chat. He also built Zealos, a Claude/Vercel/Supabase workspace combining calendars, inboxes, messages, prospect enrichment, website visitors, and outreach sequences. The revenue figures are self-reported and combine consulting, training, and custom builds.
  • How OpenAI uses ChatGPT Sites, Kath Korevec on How I AI, about 36m new.
    Summary: Kath Korevec presents ChatGPT Sites as more than a static page or prototype: it is a deployment layer for software built with Codex, handling the last mile of getting locally generated software online and shareable. The central enterprise feature is Plugins in Sites, where each visitor authorizes their own connected tools. A shared Site can therefore render different Slack, Notion, or calendar information without the creator distributing API keys or manually implementing every permission path. Korevec demonstrates an incident-command Site connected to Slack, Notion, and calendar data. It uses approved connectors and Slack context to infer a visitor's team, then presents incident timelines, people, tools, runbooks, and response guides, giving a product manager situational awareness without repeated reporting interruptions. She also treats Sites as infrastructure for disposable personal software. A “Heavy Rotation” project inspects popular Reddit playlists, runs an automation every Monday, and creates a fresh Spotify playlist while referencing local Apple Music content. A 3D dungeon crawler demonstrates how a distributed skill can define room dimensions, controls, and structural rules so other users generate themed rooms through natural-language prompts. The speakers discuss bringing inference directly into Sites so users can modify experiences in place. Korevec attributes the creative effect to model speed: slow generation breaks the builder's flow, while immediate feedback encourages experimentation. She keeps a boundary around communication: Codex may research, think, and draft, but she does not want it sending email or Slack messages in her name. Source note: the summary uses the full linked video transcript; the newsletter page itself is primarily show notes.
  • Brian Chesky: AI agents need their own OS, TechCrunch, about 12m read new.
    Summary: Brian Chesky argues that consumer AI has not solved the interface problem, especially for travel and commerce. He is skeptical that a conventional chatbot is the right surface for browsing because it presents only a few options and requires repeated turns. Travel also depends on inspiration, comparison, anticipation, and collaborative planning; people may enjoy looking through accommodations and discussing options rather than delegating the entire process to an agent. Airbnb is exploring a multiplayer interface for families and friends to plan together with AI. Chesky does not see Airbnb's current AI search interface as final. His preferred future combines deterministic and generative elements: users browse listings, compare maps, message hosts, verify identity, and hand work to an agent when appropriate. Airbnb wants to become agent-friendly because external agents could generate leads, but they need either a handoff into Airbnb or a richer SDK that brings Airbnb functionality into the agent environment. Chesky imagines Airbnb capabilities becoming specialized agents—search, customer service, and others—coordinated by a broader Airbnb agent. His central thesis is that AI needs an operating-system substrate with interoperable agents, applications, and developer tools, rather than a single “quarterback” layered onto iOS, macOS, or Windows. He rejects the idea that interfaces will disappear completely into voice and text or that all software will be dynamically generated. Apps remain important while platform owners control the transition to agent-native computing. This is a product and platform thesis in a full article, not a technical roadmap or verified launch plan.
